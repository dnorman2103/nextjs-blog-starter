TEST The cosmos is a tapestry woven with billions of galaxies, each containing billions of stars. For most of human history, we could only dream of planets orbiting these distant suns. That all changed in the 1990s, and now we know that almost every star in the sky is likely accompanied by its own planetary system. These distant worlds, known as **exoplanets**, range from 'Hot Jupiters' baked by their parent star to rocky **Super-Earths** nestled in the **habitable zone**—the orbital region where a planet's surface temperature is just right for liquid water to exist. This "Goldilocks Zone" is where the most intense searches for life are focused.

One of the most effective ways to find these elusive worlds is the **Transit Method**. When an exoplanet passes directly between its star and our telescope, it blocks a tiny fraction of the starlight. By precisely measuring this subtle dip in the star's brightness, astronomers can deduce the planet's size and orbital period. Missions like the Kepler Space Telescope and TESS have used this technique to catalogue thousands of new worlds, revealing a universe far more diverse and populated than we ever imagined.

## The Search for Biosignatures

TEST Finding a planet in the habitable zone is only the first step; the next is determining if it actually harbors life. We can't land a probe on these distant worlds, but we can study their **atmospheres**. As an exoplanet transits its star, a small amount of starlight filters through its atmosphere, leaving a spectral fingerprint. Astronomers analyze this light for **biosignatures**—gases that are strongly associated with biological processes on Earth, such as oxygen, methane, or ozone.

The powerful capabilities of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) are now being turned toward this task. By analyzing the atmospheres of rocky worlds orbiting nearby M-dwarf stars, scientists hope to find the first chemical evidence of life beyond our solar system, fundamentally changing our place in the universe.
